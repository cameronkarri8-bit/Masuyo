**Short answer:** a good website brief answers five things. What the business does, what the site needs to achieve, what already exists, what the constraints are, and what your budget is. Two pages is enough. Briefing the problem is more useful than briefing a feature list.

## Why the brief matters more than the shortlist

Most bad website projects are traceable to a vague brief rather than a bad agency. If three suppliers are guessing at what you want, you will get three quotes for three different projects and no way to compare them.

A clear brief also tells you something about the people you send it to. How they respond is the best free sample you will get.

## What to include

### 1. The business in three sentences

What you do, who you serve, and what makes you the obvious choice for them. Resist the temptation to write your whole history.

Include your location and whether you serve a local area or nationally. This has direct consequences for how the site is structured for search.

### 2. What the site needs to achieve

State this as an outcome, not a feature. "We need more qualified enquiries from people searching for our services in Lancashire" is useful. "We need a modern website" is not.

If there is a number attached, include it. Knowing you want to go from four enquiries a month to twelve changes the recommendation.

### 3. What exists now

List your current site URL, the platform it runs on, who hosts it, who owns the domain, and what you like and dislike about it.

Also flag anything that must carry over. Existing bookings, a customer database, historic blog content that still gets traffic, or integrations with software you use daily.

### 4. Scope and constraints

This is the section that prevents arguments later.

- Rough page count, or the list of pages if you know it
- Functionality you definitely need, such as bookings or payments
- Who is writing the copy
- Who is providing photography
- Any brand guidelines or assets that already exist
- Your deadline and what is driving it

### 5. Budget

Give a figure or a range. Agencies are not trying to spend all of it, they are trying to work out which of several possible projects you are asking for.

If you are unsure what is realistic, our guide to [what a website costs in the UK](/guides/website-cost-uk) sets out the bands and what sits inside each.

## What not to put in a brief

A few common inclusions actively make the brief worse.

**A list of websites you like, with no explanation.** Say what you like about them. "Clean and modern" describes almost every site built since 2018.

**A specification you are guessing at.** If you are not sure whether you need a membership area, say that. Specifying it as a requirement means you will be quoted for it.

**A demand for free ideas.** Asking for design concepts or a strategy before appointing anyone filters out the good suppliers and attracts the desperate ones.

## Reading the responses

Once the brief is out, the responses tell you more than the meetings will.

Good signs are questions that challenge your assumptions, a written scope that restates your requirements in their own words, clear ownership terms for code and domains, and a stated position on what happens after launch.

Warning signs are a quote that arrives within an hour with no questions, an unwillingness to name a price, vague deliverables such as "SEO included", and platform recommendations made before anyone has understood the requirement. On that last point, our comparison of [Next.js and WordPress](/guides/nextjs-vs-wordpress) is worth reading so you can tell whether a recommendation is reasoned or reflexive.

## Sense-check the scope before you sign

Before you accept a proposal, check it against [what a small business website should include](/guides/small-business-website-checklist). If something on that list is absent from the quote, ask whether it has been forgotten or deliberately excluded.

Then check the ongoing arrangement. Hosting, updates and support should be priced and stated, not left to be sorted out later.
