**Short answer:** most sites lose enquiries at four points. A weak service page, an unclear single action, a form that asks too much, and a slow follow-up. Fix them in that order. Traffic is rarely the actual problem for a site that already gets visitors.

## Start by ruling out the obvious

Before changing anything, send yourself a test enquiry through every form on the site. Then check it arrived, check it did not land in spam, and check the auto-reply fired.

Broken forms are the most common problem we find on existing sites, and they are usually broken silently after a plugin update or a mailbox change. It costs five minutes to rule out and occasionally solves the whole problem.

Then check your analytics are actually recording. If you cannot see which pages people land on and where they leave, everything below is guesswork.

## Fix the service pages first

Home pages get traffic, service pages get decisions. This is where the biggest gains usually sit.

Each service page should answer four questions in order. What is this, who is it for, what does it cost, and what happens next. Most pages answer the first and skip the rest.

Include pricing, or at least a range. Hiding prices does not create curiosity, it creates a shortlist you are not on. Transparency is a core part of how we operate for exactly this reason.

## One action per page

Every additional option weakens the one you actually want. A page with a contact form, a newsletter signup, a brochure download and a phone number gives visitors four ways to hesitate.

Pick the single action that matters and repeat it. We cap ourselves at two calls to action across an entire website, which forces the decision rather than deferring it.

## Shorten the form

Ask for the minimum you need to have a first conversation. Name, email and message is enough for most service businesses.

Qualifying questions belong in your reply, not in the form. Every field you add filters out people who would have become customers along with the ones you were trying to filter out.

If you genuinely need more detail, use a two-step form where the first step is short. Once someone has started, they are far more likely to finish.

## Add proof at the decision point

Testimonials on a separate page get read by almost nobody. Proof works where the decision happens, which is next to the price and next to the button.

Use specifics rather than praise. A line describing what changed and by how much beats three sentences about how lovely you were to work with.

Case studies, review counts, accreditations and named clients all work. Choose whichever you can evidence honestly.

## Speed up the follow-up

This one sits outside the website and outperforms most on-site changes.

Response time is the single biggest lever on whether an enquiry converts. Someone who submits a form has almost certainly contacted two or three competitors in the same session, and the first credible reply usually wins.

Automating the acknowledgement and getting a real reply out within the hour is achievable for almost any business. This is exactly what we built for aesthetics clinics, and the same pattern works in most service sectors.

## Check the basics underneath

None of the above matters if the fundamentals are missing. Run your site against the list in [what a small business website should include](/guides/small-business-website-checklist), particularly the sections on mobile experience and page speed.

Slow pages lose enquiries before anyone reads the copy. If your site takes more than three seconds to become usable on a phone, fix that before rewriting anything.

Conversion work is not a one-off project. The gains come from making small changes monthly and watching what happens, which is exactly what a decent maintenance arrangement should be used for.
